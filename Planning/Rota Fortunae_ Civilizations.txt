Core Mechanics
Civilizations
Civilizations are who the player actually controls and directs. Players act as the spirit of the civilization, directing countries and their parts. The primary currency is Civilizational Will or CW for short. CW is what players use to change priorities, instate new regimes, provoke revolts, push through reforms, and more against or beyond what the AI would normally do.


Civilizational Will - Primary Currency to change civilization
Treasury - Money used by you to shape matters
* Up to 20% (modified by cohesion) of your pop’s excess wealth beyond sustenance will be donated to your CT
* Charity - money given to the poorest to reduce inequality
* Entrepreneurship
Legacy - Currency & Accumulated value used to shape cultural/religious traits, scoring
Countries
A country is a temporary sovereign political unit that controls locations and governs their populations. Countries act autonomously through AI, but can be influenced & directed by player actions. They collect and spend Wealth, maintain the different aspects of the state, conduct political reforms, create elite opportunities, conduct diplomacy, and wage wars. Countries may rise, divide, merge, be conquered or disappear without destroying the host civilization.


Countries have a capital, owned & occupied territory, a primary civilization, accepted civilizations, government civics, institutions, cores and claims, diplomatic statuses, and a sovereignty status like if it’s independent, subject, or a rebel tag.


The primary civilization may choose to sponsor a country. As such, they will be able to set priorities (economic, political, expansion, etc) and will receive events when the country is at a sufficiently important crossroads. Each sponsorship takes CW to maintain

Culture
Cultural Values
Like Stellaris Ethics/EU5 Values, every culture possesses a position on seven discrete Value axes. Each has five positions. Position 3 is not neutral; it represents something closer to the broad historical human norm. Positions toward either end represent increasingly unusual cultural arrangements.


Values are relatively persistent. The player cannot casually change them. They can usually be altered through Ethnogenesis or Cultural Reform at a Legacy cost. Large or rapid changes can cause unrest and potentially contribute to cultural splitting.


Values have three major functions:
1. They directly alter how cultures behave in important systems
2. They determine Affinity with Cultural Traits and potentially country Civics/Institutions
3. They form a major component of Cultural Similarity


Traits can have both affinity preferences and hard incompatibilities. For example Aristocratic Society might strongly prefer certain Hierarchy and Authority positions, while simply being impossible alongside Libertarian Authority.


Affinity is calculated from categorical distance between a culture’s Values and the trait’s preferred positions, combined with relevant non-Value conditions. Higher affinity lowers the Legacy cost of selecting the trait and strengthens both its positive and negative effects.

Organization
The core mechanical theme is private incentive versus social obligation.

Toward Atomic/Individual, people retain more of the rewards of personal economic success. That creates stronger incentives for entrepreneurship, capital accumulation and reinvestment, but also produces a weaker social floor. Poverty, inequality, and social disruption therefore have more potential to damage cohesion.


Toward collective/communal, successful members face stronger expectations to distribute their surplus through the wider social network. Because families and individuals are not explicitly simulated, this can simply appear as a modifier to things like reinvestment rates, wealth concentration, cohesion loss, or poverty effects.


Atomic -> Higher potential dynamism, weaker automatic social resilience
Communal -> Stronger resilience and cohesion, weaker private accumulation and entrepreneurship


This can significantly affect industrialization. A communal culture interferes particularly with the ability of private actors to retain the returns on investment, one of the prereqs for sustained endogenous capitalist development. Exceptional institutional arrangements may overcome that, but it is meant to be a genuine disadvantage.


Atomic - If people manage success, then they have earned the right to keep their wealth by their own hard work and ambition. If they fall behind, well, they can pick themselves back up!
Individual - We are a collective of individuals, to whom our duties are owed. We cheer and reward those who do great works, and aid those who, by no fault of their own, cannot.
Familial - The family is the most important social unit of human civilization. Do the will of your father. Listen to the advice of your mother. And help your aunts and uncles and cousins whenever possible. And they shall help you in your time of need.
Collective - If society is to mean anything, it means that we live together, fight together, and die together. We bore you and raised you, and now you shall offer yourself to everyone else to provide for their needs as well.
Communal - We are but part of a greater whole. Individual needs and wants must be sacrificed to make society function. Our ups and downs are shared, so we can all bear the weight together.


Hierarchy
Hierarchy principally determines mobility between Pop Types:
Slaves <-> Commoners <-> Elites


Importantly, this is not an evenly spaced gradient.
Fixed -> Makes essentially all social mobility difficult
Stratified -> Strongly preserves social strata. Entry into and exit from Elite status are both difficult .
Ascribed -> maintains a meaningful difference between Elites and everyone else, but permits substantially more actual movement between social positions
Mobile -> Makes social advancement and decline much easier and treats achieved status as normal
Egalitarian -> Minimizes legitimate formal barriers between strata


Positions directly provide the base transition values, rather than applying modifiers afterward. For example, under otherwise identical conditions upward elite mobility might have values like:
Fixed 3 -> Stratified 12 -> Ascribed 25 ->...

While downward Elite mobility could have a different curve:
Fixed 4 -> Stratified 8 -> Ascribed 20 ->...

This gives both sides strategic uses. High mobility increases opportunities, improves utilization of ambitious/talented populations, and encourages advancement. But it also allows populations to become elites more quickly and therefore accelerate elite overproduction.


Low mobility can stave off elite overproduction by preventing successful non-elites from entering the elite class, while simultaneously producing frustrated aspirants and wasting human potential.


A culture’s Hierarchy Value should also affect how legitimate these barriers feel. Low mobility causes less grievance in a society that sees fixed ranks as normal than in a Mobile or Egalitarian society.


Fixed - Our place in society is given to us at birth, and there is honor in fulfilling the role into which we were born. A stable society depends upon each person knowing where they belong.
Stratified - Society functions because different classes fulfill different roles. Advancement may sometimes be deserved, but too much movement up and down the pyramid threatens the whole structure.
Ascribed - Birth and station deserve respect, but ambition must not be denied. Those who prove themselves worthy should have the opportunity to rise.
Mobile - Merit, ability, and achievement ought to determine your lot in life. Birth may provide a beginning, but it is fine if they may fall.
Egalitarian - All men are created equal. No person possesses an inherent right to stand above another merely because of birth, wealth, or station.


Authority
Authority governs the cultural legitimacy of coercive collective action.


Toward commanding/authoritative, governments can impose obligations with less cultural resistance. Towards popular/libertarian, coercive action requires progressively stronger justification and tends to create greater resistance.


This is not a government size axis. A culture can possess a large and powerful state while remaining culturally Libertarian (USA). Coercion will carry greater political/cultural costs.


Authority should interact strongly with a country Civics and Institutions. A country whose political organization conflicts sharply with its culture’s Authority  Value may be maintainable, but with increased costs.


Commanding - Those placed above us are meant to command, and those below are meant to obey. Order is preserved when everyone performs their duty without constant bickering and questioning of their betters.
Authoritative - Those entrusted with authority generally know how best to exercise it. Their judgement deserves deference, though even a rightful authority can occasionally overstep its bounds.
Statist - Public authority is necessary for an orderly society, and its legitimate commands should generally be obeyed. When power is abused, the answer is to correct the abuse, not reject authority itself.
Popular - Authority is legitimate because the people have entrusted it to those who govern. A state that loses the consent of its people loses the foundation of its right to rule.
Libertarian - Power must always justify itself before it intrudes upon the lives of ordinary people. We cannot assume that those above us will protect our interests… people must remain capable of protecting themselves and one another whether the powers at be find that acceptable or not.


Relations
Relations governs how scalable trust is.


At Kinship/Personal end, cooperation works extremely well through personal relationships, family, patronage, and direct social ties. Such societies can operate with very lightweight formal administration at small scales. The weakness appears as the polity grows. It becomes increasingly difficult to govern millions of strangers through personal relationships.


At the Contractual/Impersonal end, people are more willing to cooperate through explicit rules, offices, procedures, contracts, and institutions even when the individuals involved do not personally know one another.


The historic human baseline may sit at the extreme Kinship end here as early human political organization overwhelmingly relied upon personal and kin relationships.


Impersonal - We do not need to know one another personally in order to cooperate. If everyone follows the same trusted rules and procedures, even complete strangers can depend upon one another.
Contractual - People with different interest can still work together when their obligations are clearly agreed upon. Contracts, negotiation, and impartial judgement may very well allow mere acquaintances to cooperate without needing personal loyalty.
Reciprocal - Respect should be answered with respect, generosity with generosity, and loyalty with loyalty. Trust grows from the knowledge that what we offer others will be returned in kind.
Personal - Trust is built between people who know one another. I can depend upon you because I know your character, and you can depend upon me because you know mine.
Kinship - Those we can truly depend upon are our close of kin and those among whom we have been raised. Blood and belonging create obligations no stranger can easily replace.


Spirituality
Spirituality changes the relative importance of material conditions versus spiritual/symbolic order in satisfaction, cohesion, and legitimacy.


Sacral/Devotional cultures can tolerate relatively poor material more easily if sacred order, rituals, and religious institutions and culturally important spiritual expectations are satisfied. Their vulnerability lies in religious disruption, desecration, or lack of fulfillment of spiritual duties.


Worldly/Materialist cultures place substantially more weight on measurable material outcomes. Prosperity, consumption, services, and improving living standards become stronger sources of satisfaction and legitimacy, whilst economic deterioration becomes harder to compensate for with religion.


Spiritual societies endure poverty better, but have certain spiritual expectations.
Materialist societies exploit prosperity better, but can suffer worse in bad times.


Sacral - The sacred order of the world must be honored. Rituals must be maintained. Rulers shall follow their duties. We must stay aligned to the divine even when matters become difficult.
Devotional - Material concerns have their place, but they are temporary. What ultimately matters is our relationship with the divine and the spiritual obligations that give life meaning.
Spiritual - We are a spiritual people, and our faith and traditions remain central to who we are. Yet the needs of this world matter as well, and neither should be neglected.
Worldly - There may be realities beyond what we can see and touch, but we live and die in the material world. So while spiritual concerns deserve respect, they cannot eclipse practical every-day needs.
Materialist - What matters most is the world we can observe, understand, and improve. Spiritual questions may interest some, but they should not distract us from the realities that shape human life.


Stability
Stability is a culture’s attitude towards change, and determines cultural inertia.


Toward Continuity/Traditional, cultures preserve Traits exceptionally well. Cultural reforms are harder, foreign influence penetrates less, low-Affinity Traits persist longer, and cultural identity shifts less. Technology also spreads more slowly. However they also receive additional trait points.


Towards Adaptive/Experimental, cultures alter themselves more readily. Reform is easier, new cultural practices spread faster, and traits can respond much more dynamically to changing Affinity. This involves automatic Trait turnover. An experimental society has faster research speed and an additional trait slot.


A Trait with low Affinity may drop automatically, and vice-versa with a high Affinity Trait emerging without explicit player approval. This means Adaptive/Experimental, whilst lowering Legacy costs to reform & making it easier to occur can be genuinely bad for a player who wants to preserve an old configuration. Meanwhile Continuity may preserve traditions far beyond their original context.


Stability also affects how disruptive Culture changes are. Large jumps can instill unrest & result in culture splitting.


Continuity - The traditions of our ancestors have carried us this far and must be preserved. We are merely their custodians, entrusted to pass what we inherited onward unchanged.
Traditional - Tradition is not the worship of ashes, but the preservation of the fire. Customs may change in form when necessary, but the inheritance at their heart must endure.
Conservative - Change is dangerous when pursued carelessly, but refusing all change can leave a society too brittle to survive. We should preserve what works and alter only what necessity demands.
Adaptive - Traditions that no longer serve us should be discarded, and useful ideas should not be rejected merely because they are unfamiliar. Survival belongs to those capable of adapting without losing themselves.
Experimental - Progress cannot be made by fearing every unfamiliar path. We should test new ideas, abandon what fails, and continually seek better ways to live.


Community
Community determines the culture’s moral recognition radius. It affects which other populations are regarded as legitimate recipients of moral concern.


Parochial primarily recognizes one’s own country only.
Exclusionary extends it to the same civilization.
Particular recognizes a broader range, with an ability to “pick and choose.”
Inclusive generally recognizes outsiders as legitimate peoples, though sufficiently objectionable cultures, religions, or Traits may fall outside that boundary.
Universal recognizes all cultures.


This affects the cultural/political cost of:
* Slavery
* Forced assimilation
* Expulsion
* Persecution
* Exploitation
* Collective Punishment
* Massacres/Genocide
* Cultural Restrictions
* Treatment of conquered peoples


They do not become mechanically impossible at high Community values. Rather, carrying them out is significantly more difficult. More CW, unrest, legitimacy loss, refusal, elite opposition, etc.


Assimilation may be difficult for Parochial and Exclusionary cultures, but easy for Inclusive, though more difficult for Universal.


Parochial - Our first obligations are to ourselves. Outsiders are strangers and are not to be considered or trusted.
Exclusionary - There are peoples beyond our own whom we can recognize and respect, but most stand outside the bounds of civilized society. We owe little to those barbarians who reject the standards by which proper peoples live.
Particular - The world contains many peoples, each with their own ways and worth. Some are familiar enough to understand and respect, and the rest are either savages or too alien to be of concern.
Inclusive - Civilized life is not the possession of a single people. Many different cultures are worthy of tolerance and respect, though some are simply beyond the pale.
Universal - Every human being possesses moral worth by merit of being human. We may condemn their actions, oppose their beliefs, or even wage war against them, but they remain, ultimately, human.
Cultural Traits
While values provide the general cultural environment, traits provide the bespoke ideas that distinguish one culture from another.


Ex: Militarism, Legalism, Aristocratic Society, Go-Getter Attitude, Honor Culture, Maritime Tradition, Experimentalism, Clan Society


Traits generally require either their selection conditions to be fulfilled or for the Trait to exist in a parent culture involved in the new culture’s creation.


Each culture has a trait capacity and slots. Each trait consumes one and only one slot, but may consume capacity (or give it back) at varying rates (like Stellaris species traits). A minor trait might only be 1 point, a major one is 2, and a build-defining trait is 3. Meanwhile Burdens (negative traits) might give 1 or 2 points back.


Affinity
Traits have preferred Value positions on one or more axes. Affinity represents how intensely a culture expresses a Cultural Trait. It is derived from distance from its preferred Value position, 


Affinity affects:
* Legacy acquisition & drop cost
* Strength of Trait effects, positive and negative


High affinity isn’t simply “more bonus”, it means the culture expresses the Trait more intensely.

Cultural Evolution
Simplification: All cultural traits/values are on the civilization-level, so the only valid note here is cultural reform. Splitting and hybridization will not occur. The following is kept for safekeeping.


Values are changed principally through:


Ethnogenesis - Creation of a new daughter culture
Hybridization - Creation from two parent cultures
Cultural Reform - Deliberate restructuring of an existing culture


A cultural split inherits heavily from its parent. A hybrid must retain at least one Trait from each parent, while receiving access to both parents’ cultural Trait pools.


A major reform of a geographically enormous or internally divergent culture can itself cause cultural splitting rather than successfully transforming the whole population.


Cultures, therefore, have Heartlands which anchor identity and help determine where reform, ethnogenesis, assimilation, and cultural persistence are strongest.


Newly created cultures do not instantly replace their parents. Ethnogenesis causes a temporary period of accelerated cultural conversion that may last decades. Rival civilizations can potentially initiate competing ethnogenesis during that window (with regards to Hybridization), with the dominant emerging culture assimilating the weaker one.


Declined cultures likewise assimilate away much faster, while providing some Legacy upon retirement.

Secular Cycles
In theory, countries and/or regions are sorted into phases of what are called secular cycles. These are the following phases:
* Expansion
   * Vigorous population growth
   * Stable prices and wages
   * Few but growing Commoners, Few but growing Elites
* Stagnation
   * Population approaches carrying capacity, transition smooth
   * Majority of commoners  experience economic difficulty
   * Elites enjoy golden age
   * Many and stagnant Commoners, Many and growing Elites
* Crisis
   * Transition from stagnation can be abrupt
   * Pandemics, extreme famine, or state collapse followed by civil war
   * Extended period of time that can last one or more human generations
   * Many but shrinking Commoners, Many but shrinking Elites
* Depression
   * Smooth transition
   * Characterized by endemic civil conflict
   * Ends when elites are pruned by internal conflict and popular misery has ended so new secular cycle can begin
   * If no functioning state can reemerge, it transitions into an “intercycle”
   * Strong elites can lengthen depression (e.g. Mamaluks)
   * Few and stagnant Commoners, Many but shrinking Elites


Typical periods of secular cycles lie in the range of 2-3 centuries in monogamous strong powers. Polygamy may result in elite reproduction rates so high that commoners do not even factor into the cycle. With foreign tribes conquering and ruling a settled state, depression state may be little to none (as old elites get replaced with fewer tribal elites). For these types of states a secular cycle may take only 1 century to complete.


Secular Cycles in RF
Secular cycles are emergent historical rhythms produced by four main variables: population pressure, elite overproduction, sociopolitical instability, and state capacity. These are derived from game-state truths and turned into a 0-1 scale. The game does not force countries through fixed phases, but instead the labels describe the dominant dynamic in a society.


There are three phases:
1. Prosperity
2. Strain
3. Fracture
Prosperity
Occurs when population pressure is low, elite overproduction is low, and unrest is low. State capacity increases during this time and it is the era of a general “golden age” for the people. Wages are high, population growth is strong, and the state tends to be looking to expand & grow.
Strain
Occurs when either population pressure or elite overproduction is high. Instability tends to rise, state capacity reaches its maximum, wages drop, the population suffers, and elites have a golden age… a “Gilded Age” of sorts. High arts & culture flourishes but you increasingly suffer famines & diseases. Elite overproduction puts strain on the state and makes it increasingly belligerent.
Fracture
Occurs when population pressure or elite overproduction is high but dropping. Instability reaches its peak, state capacity drops, possibly to zero, due to unrest & maintaining elite status. Four horsemen of the Apocalypse appear. When population pressure drops too low, that squeezes elites and causes endemic civil wars until elite overproduction clears out. Population is often unable to grow due to extreme instability. State tries to restart but often fails again with repeated waves of violence (Father-son cycles).

Insurgencies & Rebellions
Resilience
Country value. Answers how much unrest each owned location can tolerate before a revolt is possible.


Determined by taking the minimum of the following:
* Legitimacy
* Elite Satisfaction
* Control (weighted avg across locations)
* Security (weighted avg across locations)
Unrest & Rebel Movements
Unrest is a location value. Determines how uppity a location is. Is not exclusive to revolts to break away but includes any popular discontent.


High unrest can cause the following problems:
* Decrease security and control in the location
* Create attritional costs to state resources
* Affect logistics for nearby military forces


When a location exceeds resilience (OR State Capacity) divided by two then a rebel movement forms.


Whether the movement is a civil (war) movement or a secessionary movement is determined by the location’s loyalty. Over 50 loyalty turns into a civil movement, under 50 is a secession movement. Loyalty itself is determined by whether there are foreign cores, civilizational distribution and attitudes, and a few other things.


Rebel movements, active on a per-country basis, track involved locations, can be interacted with directly by players, and can grow or shrink over time with eligible locations. The eligibility of a location is determined by the above conditions to create a rebel movement. Rebel movements only trigger a revolt should any location exceed the owner’s resilience.


If the average control of the rebelling locations is low enough (<25%) then the rebel movement may secede without causing a war, though the former owner will receive a casus beli. Should the average control be near zero (<10%) then they will not receive a casus beli.


If a Civil rebellion fires, and the capital province has joined the movement, has Unrest in excess of Resilience, and Elite Satisfaction is below a critical threshold, then instead of firing a civil war a Coup d’Etat occurs, instantly changing out the ruling regime. This, however, will generate a Shock event that will reduce resilience, so it may end up creating a counter-civil war.


There can also be a unique situation where low average control can result in fragmentation. How much it fragments depends on the cohesion of the population.

Economy
Goods
Goods are produced on-time. There is no stockpiling and if they are not used they are discarded.


Goods are agglomerated per location, per region (in each country), and then per country. Effective distance to the capital of the region and then to the capital of the country determines how much is transported. Trade, then, can be conducted between countries for food, with the cost of import being the price of food multiplied by effective distance.


Food
Food is literally food. It provides the baseline for carrying capacity. Grown on Cultivated Land and Raised on Pastoral Land.


Food demand is determined by the quantity of the population. High food demand relative to supply induces population stress. Having more food demand than supply creates a famine. As such, it is very important to keep food supply higher than food demand at all times, though in pre-modern societies this will be rather difficult.


Consumables
Consumables represent general urban manufactured goods. They are produced on Urban Land.


Consumables demand depends on wealth per capita. The wealthier a pop is, the more goods they will demand. Relative goods demand changes the wealth output of urban land. High goods demand increases its wealth output whilst low goods demand decreases it. In addition to that, a low or negative goods balance will make wealthier pops more upset, being compoundingly worse in materialistic societies.


Wealth
Wealth is generated by all productive activities, and represents the effective surplus generated from economic activity. In a way it represents GDP. Unlike the other two goods types, it cannot be traded directly, though it is used in trade. 


Upon production, wealth is distributed between the elites and the commoners in that location. This is apportioned by the wage share, which is itself determined by the relative amount of population compared to opportunities in the location. Slaves do not earn wages.


Wealth can either be consumed or be invested. This can be sourced from either commoners, elites, or the state. This is then divided into local investment, which seeks returns from the originating location, or mobile investment, which enters a country-wide pool and can be placed anywhere in the country. Wealth mobility is based upon tech, infrastructure, control, security, culture, and country policy. The country-wide pool then allocates investment according to expected returns with other factors like security, control, access, and other modifiers.


Whether sourced locally or nationally, wealth is invested into either Rural Capital or Urban Capital. Rural Capital boosts Cultivated & Pastoral Land output (though weighted Cultivated) and is used to maintain Cultivated Land, whilst Urban Capital is used to boost & maintain Urban Land. The amount to reinvest is determined by a few factors, notably security, country-level incentives, and culture-wide attitudes. Invested Capital may be raided during times of war.
Land
Each location has a certain amount of arable land. It gets divided into three types:
* Cultivated Land
* Pastoral Land
* Urban Land


Pastoral Land is the default land type. When cultivated or urban land is destroyed, it becomes pastoral. It sustains moderate levels of food production and very little wealth production and requires no capital for upkeep and is free to convert to.


Cultivated Land is the secondary rural land type. In order to create Cultivated Land out of Pastoral Land, wealth must be injected to create it. Cultivated Land produces large amounts of food and a moderate amount of wealth, but it does have capital upkeep. It is vulnerable to disruption in the form of looting, perpetually low security, among other things.


Urban Land is the final land type. It takes a lot of wealth to create, has a lot of capital upkeep, produces no food, but produces a lot of wealth. Urban land is the most vulnerable to disruption. It also produces goods. It can receive a proximity bonus of “economies of scale” when located near other urban centers.


To note, Urban and Cultivated Land not only have upkeep but also require a set amount of capital investment to be maintained. Technology can increase the “capital intensity” of land which will increase the benefits, maintenance, and requirements of capital.
Infrastructure
Each location also has infrastructure in ports, roads, and railways. These take large amounts of capital to build, but they reduce effective distance. Ports decrease effective distance from land-to-sea and sea-to-land. Roads and Railways reduce effective distance overland, with railways being *far* more expensive to build, *far* more effective, and more expensive to maintain but not to the degree they are more expensive to build.


Multiple levels of infrastructure can be stacked, but they lose effectiveness with each additional level. Though, to note, ports can reduce land-sea cost to near zero, at which point the cost will mostly lie in sea-to-sea costs. Railways, likewise, can reduce land-to-land connections to near-zero, but roads are far less effective.


They are maintained by the country. Lack of infrastructure maintenance will see them deteriorate over time. 


Location Tiers
Locations may be a set of different tiers: Rural, Town, City, and Metropolis.


Rural locations tend towards local investment & low migration connection (save for a lot of available land). They also cannot have Urban Land, with Urban Land requiring Town tier or higher. Otherwise Urban Capital would be the pool that then creates the Town.


Urban locations are not merely locations that happen to have urban land, but rather unlock the ability for more Urban Capital investment. Requirements include population, Urban Capital, food access, security, and more. Urban locations pool migration & investment together.


Upgrading requires exceeding the threshold and paying a one-time cost. Demotion occurs after enough capital and land is lost to demote the city.
Labor & Production
Land & Capital determine productive capacity. That determines potential jobs


Workers distributed according to pull, generated from jobs + wealth expectation + other modifiers.


Output = nonlinear scale of production (so more workers than jobs does not mean zero marginal output).
Security
Security % is the answer to the question “Can people safely live, safely live, invest, farm, trade, & store wealth here?” It effectively determines the time horizon for investment.


Low security not only acts as a gate against investment, but also as an attritional modifier on capital beyond the % of land being used for productive purposes. 90% security guards 90% of land, but any productive land beyond that faces (proportional) attrition. As such, land with low security may sustain economic activity for short periods (e.g. safe country experiencing a short brutal war, like the Franco-Prussian War), but sustained pressure may cause permanent damage.


Looting & Pillaging may raid existing capital, converting existing capital into money for the invaders (like Meiou and Taxes for EU4).


Control
Control is the effective administrative control of the state over the location. It determines how many taxes may be collected and low enough control may result in the location becoming a new country upon revolt.


Migration
Population responds to labor scarcity & on-the-ground conditions. Migration happens with two things: push & pull factors.


Push factors include:
* Wages below expectations
* Excessive population pressure
* Insecurity
* Famine
* Unrest or persecution


Pull factors include:
* Wages above expectation
* Low population pressure
* Available land
* Favorable policies


Migration is then increased/mitigated by effective distance, terrain, cultural traits, wealth of pop, country policies, and cultural similarities.


Elite Opportunities
Elites are per location, with a civilization. Elite opportunities then are generated into two pools:


Local elite opportunities are generated by the local economy. Each land type produces elite opportunities. These go to local elites, with the civilizational distribution reflecting the local population, laws, and cultural values.


State elite opportunities are generated by the country via patronage, bureaucracy, and the military. They are nationally funded and nationally competitive, with access favoring the primary civilization though the degree other civ pops can take advantage depending on values. 


As such, on the location level, elite opportunities = local opportunities + accessible state opportunities. But the access weight would depend on civilization eligibility, control, and geographic modifiers (capital location/region).

Warfare
What is the smallest military model that makes empires rise, overextend, fracture, and leave successors?


Three main concepts:
1. Force - How much violence the state can project
2. Logistics - How far the state can effectively project its violence 
3. Pressure - How much violence the country must absorb or suppress


The thinking should be “My empire is powerful near the core, weak at the frontiers, and too expensive to hold together.”


Army - How much land power the country has
* Has a base strength value
* Deteriorates with distance
Navy - How much sea power the country has
* Extends reach across sea zones (decreasing effective distance)
* Protects coastal commerce & security
* Enables overseas war
Logistics - How far the country can project power
* Deployment consumes a certain amount of logistics
* Logistic cost = amount * distance. 
* A small expeditionary force halfway across the world may be more expensive than sustaining a large force nearby.
* Individual for each theater commitment, but summed for general territory maintenance
* More logistics is burdened, the worse that sustaining long-distance operations gets. (So expeditionaries are disproportionately affected)
* Very likely logistics will be overburdened in most circumstances until the industrial era
* Sourced from infrastructure, military institutions, doctrine, and industrial base. Logistics cost may be reduced from local support & bases
Military Burden - How expensive the military is relative to the state
* Should be clear how much each operation costs
* More logistics strain = more costly
* Maintaining land requires a certain amount of army, naval power and logistics
* Inability to sustain the necessary burden results in security & control issues


Map
Instead of armies as pieces, you have a military control overlay where each empire exerts pressure.
Every location has a state:
* Protected
* Exposed
* Overextended
* Contested
* Uncontrolled
The more a country tries to claim, the more their logistics & army get stretched. Pressure can have meaningful effects on the location’s security. It may even exude into adjacent territory depending on the situation (e.g. if that country claims the territory, nomad states, so on).


To prevent blobbing, things like power getting weaker the further you are from the core, the fact every frontier creates additional burden, and that conquest should be easy but it's more expensive to hold territory.

Wars & Theaters
A theater is a region where military force is actively contested. They can be created by:
* Contested border region
* Rebellions
* Invasions/Naval Landings
* Civil Wars
* Normal Wars
Countries commit forces to theaters. Players may influence or override country behavior with CW, but normal AI handles it.


There are two main layers:
1. Attritional Warfare
Daily and monthly background fighting. This can slowly change the front over time, but isn’t naturally decisive. It favors the “status quo”, defined as the current balance of power (this doesn’t mean the front-line doesn’t move, but rather the current momentum stays roughly the same).
2. Major Battles
These are decisive engagements where both sides commit heavily and the result may massively swing the result. Ideally there might be anywhere between 2 to 12 per theater. Small wars may be decided by one battle. Battles do not *have* to be turning points (e.g. Verdun) but should be most of the time.


In theaters with naval warfare, in order to exact landings, naval superiority must be achieved. Theaters should also remain focused, but general enough so you don’t end up with dozens of them. For a WW2 example, Europe would comprise the North African theater, Italian theater, French/Western theater, the short-lived Polish theater, the short-lived Balkan theater (for the invasion of Yugoslavia & Greece), and the Eastern Front theater. Theaters should also be allowed to merge, though with some “stickiness” as to the splitting/joining of theaters to prevent theaters from constantly splitting/rejoining with minor frontline changes. The Pacific might turn into a Burmese Theater, a China theater, a Manchuria theater, an Indonesian/South Pacific theater, and a Central/North Pacific theater. So whilst the Pacific example may have a South Pacific and North Pacific theaters that would be adjacent, they’d be too large to have as one large theater. Burma and China would also be separate due to terrain. Manchuria, finally, because of a separate war.


Each theater would have a commitment level, which determines how much army/navy capacity is sent, how much risk is accepted, and how vulnerable other areas become. The commitment level would depend on war aims & reasons


War Diplomacy
War Aims & War Reasons are state-machines designed to help the AI figure out what they want and how much they’re willing to commit to a war.


AIs should not ask “Can I win” but rather “Is it still worth it given what I’m aiming for?”. Escalation can occur by adding aims which can escalate commitment by causing reevaluation of the war reasons. Partial success may impact legitimacy for the one who over-promised. To prevent overcommitment, particularly by the AI, there would be an amount the AI would be willing to actually commit. A cost they’re willing to bear out. 


There are three main pieces:
* War Aims (What are the aims of the war. Conquest, raiding, etc)
* War Reasons (Why are we fighting? Existential, monetary gain, etc)
* Commitment Level (How much are we putting into this war)


War Aims & Reasons are static, pending player interference. But commitment level can be dynamic. If an AI is losing a war, then it will evaluate whether increasing its commitment is worth the cost, and it will evaluate it against the reason. So if an AI is just seeking loot, then it may not increase commitment. Higher commitment makes an AI not merely more willing to commit troops, but also take financial & political risks like going into debt or raising taxes.

Innovation
Innovation is a civilization-level mechanic. It is accumulated from every source and the player chooses a technology to research from among a set of weighted random choices (Basically the Stellaris system). Each tech may have a variety of bonuses to accelerate or slow down research. 


There is also a “tech spread” slot which will randomly pick a technology you have not researched and someone close-by has researched and begin automatically researching it for you. Diffusion does speed up through the game, but being in contact with a source may lend you that tech within 50 years. 


Ages
Ages are categories of proverbial breakthroughs in sociological and technological progress. Each has a set of starting & spread conditions. 




Ages:
* Antiquity (1000 BC - ~400 AD)
   * Early Game starting age
   * City States & Early Empires
   * Axial Religions
* Imperiality (~500 BC - ~1500 AD)
   * Polity scaling
   * Logistics
   * Administration
   * Pre-Gunpowder Military
* Commerciality (~1000 AD - ~1800 AD)
   * Economic scaling
   * Better economics
   * Ocean-going sailing vessels
* Modernity (1600 AD - present)
   * Breakaway - traditional barriers get broken down
   * Begins with scientific advancement
   * By Industrial Revolution, encompasses all areas


To unlock Imperiality, either you need a country with more than 1 million people or you need to be Buddhist. It has no spread conditions (so it spreads to anyone who can reach you).


To unlock Commerciality, you need either the Meritocracy or Education techs AND conduct significant trade. It does not spread to Nomads.


To unlock Modernity, you need Experimentalism, Fallibility, and Laws of Nature cultural traits and the Metallurgy, Global Trade, and Newspapers technologies. You also need 50% literacy, Centers of Learning, Independent Scholarship in any country. It can only spread to settled states.
