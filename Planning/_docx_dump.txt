207	Secular Cycles
208	Countries are sorted into phases of what are called secular cycles. These are the following phases:
209	Expansion
210	Vigorous population growth
211	Stable prices and wages
212	Few but growing Commoners, Few but growing Elites
213	Stagnation
214	Population approaches carrying capacity, transition smooth
215	Majority of commoners  experience economic difficulty
216	Elites enjoy golden age
217	Many and stagnant Commoners, Many and growing Elites
218	Crisis
219	Transition from stagnation can be abrupt
220	Pandemics, extreme famine, or state collapse followed by civil war
221	Extended period of time that can last one or more human generations
222	Many but shrinking Commoners, Many but shrinking Elites
223	Depression
224	Smooth transition
225	Characterized by endemic civil conflict
226	Ends when elites are pruned by internal conflict and popular misery has ended so new secular cycle can begin
227	If no functioning state can reemerge, it transitions into an “intercycle”
228	Strong elites can lengthen depression (e.g. Mamaluks)
229	Few and stagnant Commoners, Many but shrinking Elites
230	
231	Typical periods of secular cycles lie in the range of 2-3 centuries in monogamous strong powers. Polygamy may result in elite reproduction rates so high that commoners do not even factor into the cycle. With foreign tribes conquering and ruling a settled state, depression state may be little to none (as old elites get replaced with fewer tribal elites). For these types of states a secular cycle may take only 1 century to complete.
232	
233	Secular Cycles in RF
234	Secular cycles are emergent historical rhythms produced by four main variables: population pressure, elite overproduction, sociopolitical instability, and state capacity. These are derived from game-state truths and turned into a 0-1 scale. The game does not force countries through fixed phases, but instead the labels describe the dominant dynamic in a society.
235	
236	There are three phases:
237	Prosperity
238	Strain
239	Fracture
240	Prosperity
241	Occurs when population pressure is low, elite overproduction is low, and unrest is low. State capacity increases during this time and it is the era of a general “golden age” for the people. Wages are high, population growth is strong, and the state tends to be looking to expand &amp; grow.
242	Strain
243	Occurs when either population pressure or elite overproduction is high. Instability tends to rise, state capacity reaches its maximum, wages drop, the population suffers, and elites have a golden age… a “Gilded Age” of sorts. High arts &amp; culture flourishes but you increasingly suffer famines &amp; diseases. Elite overproduction puts strain on the state and makes it increasingly belligerent.
244	Fracture
245	Occurs when population pressure or elite overproduction is high but dropping. Instability reaches its peak, state capacity drops, possibly to zero, due to unrest &amp; maintaining elite status. Four horsemen of the Apocalypse appear. When population pressure drops too low, that squeezes elites and causes endemic civil wars until elite overproduction clears out. Population is often unable to grow due to extreme instability. State tries to restart but often fails again with repeated waves of violence (Father-son cycles).

260	Insurgencies &amp; Rebellions
261	Resilience
262	Country value. Answers how much unrest each owned location can tolerate before a revolt is possible.
263	
264	Determined by taking the minimum of the following:
265	Legitimacy
266	Elite Satisfaction
267	Control (weighted avg across locations)
268	Security (weighted avg across locations)
269	
270	Certain “shock” events may change resilience temporarily. These can induce revolts by lowering the threshold enough to make them possible.
271	Ex: Succession Crisis: -24 Resilience, improves by 12 every year
272	
273	Unrest &amp; Rebel Movements
274	Unrest is a location value. Determines how uppity a location is. Is not exclusive to revolts to break away but includes any popular discontent.
275	
276	The Unrest Threshold is a country value. Determines at what point does unrest start to affect the location. The owner can only react to unrest once it reaches this point. Higher unrest thresholds, therefore, result in less problems initially, but offer less time for the country to react to developing situations. Higher unrest thresholds also worsen the effects of unrest, should it go beyond the unrest threshold. 
277	
278	High unrest can cause the following problems:
279	Decrease security and control in the location
280	Create attritional costs to state resources
281	Affect logistics for nearby military forces
282	
283	When a location exceeds either
284	Resilience divided by 2
285	Country’s unrest threshold
286	Then a rebel movement forms.
287	
288	Whether the movement is a civil (war) movement or a secessionary movement is determined by the location’s loyalty. Over 50 loyalty turns into a civil movement, under 50 is a secession movement. Low loyalty increases unrest in a location. High cohesion of the same civilization pops increases loyalty and low cohesion lowers loyalty. High cohesion of different civilizations’ pops decreases loyalty and low cohesion does not affect loyalty. Loyalty is capped at 50% + ½ * your own civilization's population share in the location.
289	
290	Rebel movements, active on a per-country basis, track involved locations, can be interacted with directly by players, and can grow or shrink over time with eligible locations. The eligibility of a location is determined by the above conditions to create a rebel movement. Rebel movements only trigger a revolt should any location exceed the owner’s resilience.
291	
292	If the average control of the rebelling locations is low enough (&lt;25%) then the rebel movement may secede without causing a war, though the former owner will receive a casus beli. Should the average control be near zero (&lt;10%) then they will not receive a casus beli.
293	
294	If a Civil rebellion fires, and the capital province has joined the movement, has Unrest in excess of Resilience, and Elite Satisfaction is below a critical threshold, then instead of firing a civil war a Coup d’Etat occurs, instantly changing out the ruling regime. This, however, will generate a Shock event that will reduce resilience, so it may end up creating a counter-civil war.

590	Warfare (Simple)
591	What is the smallest military model that makes empires rise, overextend, fracture, and leave successors?
592	
593	Three main concepts:
594	Force - How much violence the state can project
595	Logistics - How far the state can effectively project its violence 
596	Pressure - How much violence the country must absorb or suppress
597	
598	The thinking should be “My empire is powerful near the core, weak at the frontiers, and too expensive to hold together.”
599	
600	Army - How much land power the country has
601	Has a base strength value
602	Deteriorates with distance
603	Navy - How much sea power the country has
604	Extends reach across sea zones (decreasing effective distance)
605	Protects coastal commerce &amp; security
606	Enables overseas war
607	Logistics - How far the country can project power
608	Deployment consumes a certain amount of logistics
609	Logistic cost = amount * distance. 
610	A small expeditionary force halfway across the world may be more expensive than sustaining a large force nearby.
611	Individual for each theater commitment, but summed for general territory maintenance
612	More logistics is burdened, the worse that sustaining long-distance operations gets. (So expeditionaries are disproportionately affected)
613	Very likely logistics will be overburdened in most circumstances until the industrial era
614	Sourced from infrastructure, military institutions, doctrine, and industrial base. Logistics cost may be reduced from local support &amp; bases
615	Military Burden - How expensive the military is relative to the state
616	Should be clear how much each operation costs
617	More logistics strain = more costly
618	Maintaining land requires a certain amount of army, naval power and logistics
619	Inability to sustain the necessary burden results in security &amp; control issues
620

624	Protected

629	The more a country tries to claim, the more their logistics &amp; army get stretched. Pressure can have meaningful effects on the location’s security. It may even exude into adjacent territory depending on the situation (e.g. if that country claims the territory, nomad states, so on).

657	There are two main layers:
658	Attritional Warfare
659	Daily and monthly background fighting. This can slowly change the front over time, but isn’t naturally decisive. It favors the “status quo”, defined as the current balance of power (this doesn’t mean the front-line doesn’t move, but rather the current momentum stays roughly the same).
660	Major Battles
661	These are decisive engagements where both sides commit heavily and the result may massively swing the result. Ideally there might be anywhere between 2 to 12 per theater. Small wars may be decided by one battle. Battles do not *have* to be turning points (e.g. Verdun) but should be most of the time.
662	
663	In theaters with naval warfare, in order to exact landings, naval superiority must be achieved. Theaters should also remain focused, but general enough so you don’t end up with dozens of them. For a WW2 example, Europe would comprise the North African theater, Italian theater, French/Western theater, the short-lived Polish theater, the short-lived Balkan theater (for the invasion of Yugoslavia &amp; Greece), and the Eastern Front theater. Theaters should also be allowed to merge, though with some “stickiness” as to the splitting/joining of theaters to prevent theaters from constantly splitting/rejoining with minor frontline changes. The Pacific might turn into a Burmese Theater, a China theater, a Manchuria theater, an Indonesian/South Pacific theater, and a Central/North Pacific theater. So whilst the Pacific example may have a South Pacific and North Pacific theaters that would be adjacent, they’d be too large to have as one large theater. Burma and China would also be separate due to terrain. Manchuria, finally, because of a separate war.
664	
665	Each theater would have a commitment level, which determines how much army/navy capacity is sent, how much risk is accepted, and how vulnerable other areas become. The commitment level would depend on war aims &amp; reasons (explained in the complex section - simple model would use it too).
666	Warfare (Complex)
667	Armies
668	Core Design problem:
669	Preindustrial Warfare wants discrete armies, sieges, raids, and set-piece battles
670	Modern Warfare wants fronts, logistics, mobilization, and attrition
671	Countries should not operate 100+ units by late game
672	Fully abstracting armies feels cheap
673	
674	Solution: Armies exist on the map, but exact tile/position is not sacred.
675	They exist as operational commitments tied to space, not chess pieces
676	Armies are a named formation, visible on the map, anchored in a location. They are able to move, siege, intercept, suppress, raid, and occupy, BUT they project influence and control into nearby locations. It’s not merely in “province X” but rather exists in a “quantum field” around that location.
677	
678	Forts, cities, ports, mountain passes sit in locations. Roads and sea routes connect locations. Armies move between locations. So on so forth. Battles happen *between locations*. Sieges happen *at locations*. Late-game frontlines emerge implicitly from overlapping zones of control rather than a front system or individual units in each tile.
679	
680	Three main combat contexts:
681	Location Combat
682	Sieges &amp; Assaults, Urban Warfare
683	Battles happen in one location.
684	Thinking about removing this and focusing on 2 and 3
685	Between-Location Combat
686	Intercepts, set-piece battles, frontline warfare
687	Attacker &amp; Defender occupy two different locations in battle
688	Area-of-Operations Pressure
689	Raiding, occupation spread, control projection, rebel suppression, screening, supply disruption, air superiority
690	Happens in the zone of control of armies. Relatively diffuse, and more about attrition than set-piece battles.
691	
692	Battles are operational outcomes, not tactics. They do not need to model exact flanking, formations, or tactical placement. They only need to account for army comp, tech, doctrine, terrain, supply, morale, cohesion, professionalism.
693	
694	Logistics constrains how far armies can operate, how large an army can be in a certain area, how much attrition armies suffer. Supply origin would be core homeland territory, not just the capital, so surrounding the capital does not kill all logistics.
695	
696	Navies
697	Navies at sea are equivalent to armies on land, but focus on sea-lane control:
698	Protecting naval shipping
699	Blockading enemies
700	Enable overseas invasions
701	Suppress or engage in piracy/raiding
702	Opening up new trade routes
703	
704	War Diplomacy
705	War Aims &amp; War Reasons are state-machines designed to help the AI figure out what they want and how much they’re willing to commit to a war.
706	
707	AIs should not ask “Can I win” but rather “Is it still worth it given what I’m aiming for?”. Escalation can occur by adding aims which can escalate commitment by causing reevaluation of the war reasons. Partial success may impact legitimacy for the one who over-promised. To prevent overcommitment, particularly by the AI, there would be an amount the AI would be willing to actually commit. A cost they’re willing to bear out.

778	Innovation
779	Innovation is a civilization-level mechanic. It is accumulated from every source and the player chooses a technology to research from among a set of weighted random choices (Basically the Stellaris system). Each tech may have a variety of bonuses to accelerate or slow down research. 
780	
781	There is also a “tech spread” slot which will randomly pick a technology you have not researched and someone close-by has researched and begin automatically researching it for you. Diffusion does speed up through the game, but being in contact with a source may lend you that tech within 50 years.
